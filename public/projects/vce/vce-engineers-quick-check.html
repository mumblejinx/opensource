Volta Confluence Engine — A One-Sitting Feasibility Read Before You Open the Full Guide
Does the physics hold up?
Is it worth building?
CC BY-NC

This is a one-sitting feasibility read for an engineer. The first half asks the only question that matters up front — does the physics hold up well enough to be worth anyone's time? The second half is the cost, time, and skill reality: if it works, is it worth building? Every claim carries a certainty label so you can see at a glance where the ground is solid and where it is genuinely untested.

Read this first. This design was conceived by an artist, not an engineer, and documented with discipline — but not an engineer's instincts. Considerable due diligence has gone into it, and every figure is labelled for what it is: Confirmed physics or catalogue fact, Calculated from those, or Estimated engineering judgement. What the author cannot do is check the work the way you can. That is the entire purpose of this document: to put the reasoning in front of someone qualified to find the flaw. If the physics does not hold, this project wants to know before anyone spends money — please say so.
Part 1 · The Physics

Can It Get Off the Ground?

1 · The energy balance — the make-or-break number

Everything hinges on one question: can a human hand-crank actually supply the power the instrument draws, continuously, through a performance? This is the tightest margin in the design and the first thing to check.

Power available
~31 W sustained one-hand crank (95% of population; literature reports 54 ± 14 W for young adult males) Confirmed
Power required
18–23 W continuous system draw Calculated
Margin
~31 W against ~20 W. Positive, but modest — roughly 1.3–1.7×, not a comfortable multiple.

The energy is buffered so cranking and playback need not be simultaneous: the generator produces 24 V AC, a passive Cockcroft-Walton multiplier steps it to 600 V DC, and a ceramic capacitor bank stores 4,000–5,000 J. Calculated At 20 W draw a full bank yields ~250 seconds (~4 minutes) of play, so the operator re-cranks every 3–4 minutes. Calculated Ceramic dielectric self-discharge is negligible over performance timescales, which is why a capacitor bank is used instead of batteries or supercapacitors. Estimated

The one thing most likely to sink it

The 31 W figure is comfortable cranking in isolation. Nobody has measured whether that margin survives an operator who is also concentrating on a technically demanding DJ set for 45+ minutes — cognitive load and fatigue both eat into available power. This is the design's single largest open question, and it cannot be resolved on paper. If you doubt one thing, doubt this — and it is exactly what first-prototype Experiment 1 is built to measure.

2 · The signal chain — the part you can verify in minutes

This is the most conventional element of the whole instrument: a standard two-stage valve phono preamplifier with passive RIAA, feeding transformer volume controls. The gain arithmetic is checkable against ordinary practice.

StageElementGain / effect
1MC cartridge0.2–0.5 mV output
2Step-up transformer (Lundahl LL1931, 1:10)+20 dB
312AX7 triode, first stage+26 dB
4Passive RIAA network (IEC 60098)−20 dB @ 1 kHz
512AX7 triode, second stage+26 dB
Net cartridge → line~+52 dB → 300–500 mV line level Calculated
6–8Transformer volume controls + equal-power crossfader (passive)Attenuation only; ~0 added noise
9–10Output transformer → field coil compression drivers + hornImpedance match → acoustic output

The arithmetic: +20 − (RIAA insertion) + 26 − 20 + 26 ≈ +52 dB, lifting a ~0.3 mV cartridge signal to line level. Placing RIAA between the two gain stages (not at the input) is deliberate — it keeps the first stage's noise performance intact. All of this is textbook valve-phono engineering; if something here is wrong, it should be quick for you to spot. Confirmed — standard practice

3 · Why 20 watts can fill a room

The apparent paradox — concert volume from a hand-crank — resolves at the speakers. The field coil compression drivers on a corner-loaded horn have a sensitivity of 108–112 dB/W/m Confirmed — driver class, among the highest achievable. At that efficiency, single-digit acoustic watts produce 100–112 dB SPL average in a suitable room. Estimated The whole instrument is an exercise in efficiency: high-sensitivity horns are what make the tiny power budget viable, and they are the reason the energy balance in Section 1 is merely tight rather than impossible.

5 · Where the physics is genuinely untested

Five bench experiments are specified to convert estimate into measurement. Running them first would raise first-prototype confidence from 88% to an estimated 93–95%. They are, in priority order:

#ExperimentResolves
1Generator output under a simulated 90-minute performance with deliberate cognitive loadThe energy-balance question above — the big one
2Spring motor run-time, full wind, representative platter load, 5 consecutive cyclesWhether one wind truly covers an LP side
3Mu-metal shielding, gaussmeter at cartridge with bearing energisedWhether magnetic hum stays below the noise floor
4Crossfader equal-power law and channel isolation, 1 kHzWhether the passive mix behaves
5Full signal-chain noise floor, A-weightedWhether the system is quiet enough

9 · The hard limits you cannot engineer away

These are not defects to be fixed — they are the instrument's fundamental character. Accept them or walk away.

F1The operator supplies all energy, continuously — ~20 minutes of cranking per 2-hour performance.
F2Valve warm-up: 60–90 seconds before play can begin.
F3Spring motor rewind between LP sides — ~25–28 minutes of run per wind.
F4The corner horn needs a real 90° room corner to load correctly — a venue constraint.

10 · Known risks, managed by design

Five engineering problems are identified and each has a documented mitigation — worth reviewing in full in the complete guide (Section XI): 600 V safety (E1, contained by enclosure), generator-to-cartridge EMI (E2, mu-metal shielding), the quartz PLL being post-Victorian technology (E3, a philosophical rather than functional issue), unipivot tonearm azimuth variation (E4), and field-coil supply sensitivity (E5).

Build it if…

  • You have or can commission high-voltage, machining, clockmaking, speaker, and cabinet skills
  • You can absorb $34k–$80k and 6–12 months
  • You accept F1–F4 as the instrument's nature, not flaws
  • You are drawn to being the first to prove (or disprove) the concept in the real world

Walk away if…

  • You need a working DJ tool rather than an engineering project
  • High-voltage work is outside your competence and you can't partner for it
  • The tight energy margin (Section 1) is a dealbreaker until measured
  • You cannot commit the lead time for the spring-motor commission
If you found a flaw: that is a successful use of this document. The design is open (CC BY-NC) precisely so that qualified eyes can catch what a non-engineer could not.
