The Machine That
Runs on You

A DJ Mixer That Needs No Electricity — And Why It Hasn't Been Built Yet
General-audience article
About 5 minutes
CC BY-NC

Every DJ setup in the world needs a wall outlet. It's the first thing anyone looks for walking into a venue — so obvious a requirement that nobody thinks of it as a requirement at all. It's just part of what the equipment is.

So here is a strange question: what would it take to build one that didn't need the outlet at all? Not a battery-powered version, not a quieter generator humming offstage — a two-channel vinyl mixer that runs on nothing but the effort of the person operating it, and still fills a room at concert volume.

That machine is called the Volta Confluence Engine, and it has been designed in complete engineering detail. Every dimension, every component, every circuit. It has just never been built. This is the story of what it is, why each of its unusual choices is actually the sensible one, and why "never been built" is where the interesting part begins rather than ends.

The Idea

A record player that runs on you

The whole machine is built around one constraint: the only energy allowed in is human effort. Everything else follows from that.

You start by turning a crank. That crank spins a generator, the same basic device as the one in a wind-up emergency radio, just larger. But you can't power a sensitive audio system directly from a hand crank — the moment your cranking speed wavered, the sound would waver with it. So the electricity goes somewhere to be stored and smoothed out first.

That somewhere is a capacitor bank — and the easiest way to picture it is to reach back to a school science demonstration. A Leyden jar is a jar that stores up a static-electric charge until you touch it and it discharges in a snap. A capacitor is the same idea, refined: a device that holds an electrical charge until it's needed. The Volta Confluence Engine's bank is essentially a large, carefully engineered array of these, holding enough charge to run the electronics through a performance. You crank to fill the reservoir; the music drinks from the reservoir at a steady rate. The two don't have to happen in perfect lockstep, which is what makes the whole thing playable.

From there the stored electricity runs the parts that turn a faint groove-signal into sound loud enough to dance to: valve amplifiers (the glowing glass tubes prized in vintage hi-fi, the technology that amplified nearly every recording made before the 1970s) and horn speakers (the flared wooden shape of an old gramophone, which is one of the most efficient ways ever found to turn a small electrical signal into a large amount of sound — efficiency that matters enormously when every watt came from someone's arm).

And the turntables themselves? They don't touch the electrical system at all. They spin on wind-up spring motors, completely mechanical, like a music box or an old wristwatch. The records turn whether the electronics are running or not.

The Clever Parts

Why the strange choices are the right choices

At first glance a few of these decisions look eccentric — antique, even. They aren't. Each one is the answer to a specific problem that the obvious modern choice would get wrong.

Why a capacitor bank instead of batteries? Because this instrument spends most of its life sitting in storage between performances, and batteries hate that. A charged battery — and even more so a supercapacitor, the modern high-capacity cousin — slowly leaks its charge whether you use it or not, losing a meaningful fraction every day. The ceramic capacitor bank chosen here holds its charge for a very long time, and just as importantly it needs no active electronics to manage it. Fewer parts humming and switching in the background means a cleaner, quieter path for the music.

Why an 18th-century clockmaking mechanism to spin the records? A plain wind-up spring has a problem: it pulls hard when fully wound and weakly as it runs down, so the turntable would start fast and gradually slow — ruinous for music. The solution was worked out in 1739 by John Harrison, the clockmaker who solved the problem of keeping accurate time at sea. His remontoire is a mechanism that delivers perfectly even force regardless of how wound-up the spring behind it is. The same trick that kept a ship's chronometer steady on the open ocean keeps these records turning at exactly constant speed. It was the right answer 286 years ago and it's still the right answer now.

There's a pattern in these decisions worth naming: at each point the design reaches for whatever solution actually fits the constraint best, whether that solution is old or new. A voltage-multiplying circuit from the 1930s, a speaker principle from the 1900s, a clockmaker's mechanism from the 1700s, all serving a thoroughly modern instrument. The machine is self-consistent — every part earns its place by the same logic.

The Honest Part

It hasn't been built yet

Here is the part most projects would bury, stated plainly instead: no one has ever physically built this machine. Everything described here exists as a complete, rigorous design — but a design is not a working object, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise.

So how confident is the designer that it would actually work if someone built it? About 88 percent — meaning the engineering strongly suggests it would work, but there's real uncertainty that only a physical build can resolve. Throughout the full documentation, every single figure is tagged to show whether it's a confirmed fact of physics, a careful calculation, or an educated estimate. Nothing is dressed up as more certain than it is.

The biggest genuine unknown is beautifully human: can a person keep cranking steadily enough, for a whole set, while also concentrating on actually DJing? The numbers are encouraging — a comfortable sustained crank produces more than double the power the system draws — but nobody knows what that margin feels like forty-five minutes into a demanding performance. That's not a question you can answer at a desk. You answer it by building the thing and playing it.

That's why the unbuilt status is the interesting part rather than a disappointment. This is a finished engineering answer to a question almost nobody has bothered to ask, sitting and waiting for the first person with the skills and the workshop to find out whether the answer is right.

Before You Build — Please Read This design was conceived by an artist, not an engineer. It has been documented with discipline, and every figure is labelled for what it is — Confirmed physics, Calculated, or Estimated judgement — but it has never been built, and the author cannot check the engineering the way a qualified engineer can. What you'll find here is honest reasoning offered for scrutiny, not a proven machine. It involves 600 V electronics and precision machining; anyone building it does so at their own risk. If you find a flaw, that's the point — please say so.
The Invitation

Free for anyone to build

The complete design — every specification, every drawing, the parts list, the machining notes, the testing procedures — is released free under an open licence (Creative Commons, non-commercial). Anyone with the skills and resources is welcome to build it, study it, or improve on it. The only thing asked is that it not be sold for profit.

It is a demanding build: high-voltage electronics, precision machining, a commission to a clockmaker, fine cabinetmaking in walnut and brass. It is emphatically not a weekend project. But the first person who completes one will have something that has never existed anywhere in the world before — and will, in the process, answer the one question the designer couldn't.

The full documentation lives at mumblejinx.github.io/opensource.
